FieldTest Recorder Architecture

A digital companion for NCB's own Narcotic Drugs Detection Kit. It is designed to guide the officer through the kit's Tests A–E and read each reaction against a blank well in the same photo and a published, signed colour standard. It would report the result with every substance on the kit chart that the colour is consistent with, and produce a signed, tamper-evident, per-package record with time, GPS and officer that an independent expert can verify offline.

Target design, not a description of what is built. Only the Python reference prototype exists today; there is no Android app. Several statements on this page are design only, including: the Field Test Memo, Form-1 item 5 and BSA s.63 outputs; reading tube tests (B, E); liveness and night mode inside the capture pipeline; hardware-backed keys on a real phone; the attestation checks of app signing certificate and certificate validity dates; time, binding-validity and revocation checks in the offline verifier; the verifier re-running the colour measurement. The repository README says which parts exist, and open defects are in the problem register (U27–U34 come from an independent audit on 30 Sep 2026).

This is the single current version. What earlier rounds got wrong, and what changed, is recorded in Phase 14. Revision 7 applies an external review whose every claim we re-checked. Every rate comes from code in ftr-reference/, with a 95% interval. Evidence trail: ftr-reference/docs/research/.

Summary

The answer first

The actual problem

NCB needs a field kit test to leave a record a third party can trust. The record should show that this package was tested at this place and time by this officer, what it showed, and that nothing was changed or left out afterwards. The reading should not depend on who looks at it or under what light. The Bombay High Court found NCB "has not prescribed the standards" and called field testing "arbitrary" [S36]. Identifying the drug is the lab's job, and NCB says so explicitly.

Root cause

The record is made separately from the event. The colour is judged by eye against a printed chart, with no measured standard. Nothing binds the result to the numbered package, the witnesses or the time, and no lab result ever comes back to show how often field tests are wrong.

What earlier plans got wrong

Each of these was found by experiment or review and fixed:

  • naming a drug where the chemistry only gives a colour group (E6);
  • JPEG capture for saturated blues (E12);
  • deleting records before backup (E14);
  • designing around US reagents when NCB's own kit chart was in our sources;
  • single-point colour rules that fail when the sample amount changes (E17);
  • a record format that didn't sign time and GPS (review).

The derived solution

An offline Android tool, built as an embeddable SDK:

  • it walks the officer through NCB's kit, Tests A–E in the printed flow-chart order;
  • it photographs each reaction beside a reagent-only blank well, on a reference card;
  • it reads the colour against signed colour bands built from the kit's own ranges;
  • it combines results across tests and warns when the NDPS quantity category can't be told.

Each result becomes a device-signed, chained record, anchored in the witness-signed panchnama. A thin server enrols devices, stores records and closes the Rule 14 lab loop.

Core insight. A colour test identifies a colour group, not a drug, and NCB's own kit says so: Test E's blue means "cocaine or methaqualone". A reading only means something against a documented standard, and the record is only trustworthy when bound to the witnessed seizure.

AI. No model runs on the phone. Decisions use colour bands computed from the kit chart and published physics, which anyone can recompute. Learned region maps stay paused for NCB's kit until real validation data exists.

Build for SIH: guided test → capture with blank → colour band → sign → anchor → verify, with a live tamper demo. Don't build: blockchain, microservices, a chatbot, a CNN drug classifier, or maps and heatmaps.

Core insight

A colour test identifies a colour group, not a drug

NCB's Narcotic Drugs Detection Kit states its results as colour ranges, and some ranges cover more than one drug [S6]:

  • Test E's blue is "positive for cocaine and methaqualone"; reagents E3/E4 then tell them apart.
  • Test A's purples cover morphine, codeine and heroin.

The US standard (NIJ Std-0604.01) shows the same thing: cocaine and ephedrine give the identical colour [S35].

So the app never says "cocaine" from one test. It says: positive, consistent with cocaine or methaqualone. After E3/E4 it narrows to one, and it warns when the candidates fall under different NDPS quantity thresholds.

Colour depth also changes with how much sample went in. So each outcome is a band spanning the chart's range and realistic amounts, read against a reagent-only blank in the same photo.

The Delhi High Court held that a missing field test doesn't sink a case once the lab confirms [S37]. The record therefore matters most before the lab result: at seizure, remand, bail, charge-sheet completeness and Rule 10(2) sampling. In the Anuraj case, "MDMA" turned out to be methamphetamine [S39], which puts the accused in a different quantity category.

Every kit test becomes a guided, measured reading against a published, signed colour standard, and every result a verifiable report that anyone can re-check offline.

Officer workflow

One seizure, several packages, one or more phones

  1. Open the operation: crime number, kit batch, optional e-Sakshya reference. Team phones join by QR.
  2. Choose the nature of the material (powder, tablet, herb, paper). The app picks the first test from NCB's flow chart and shows its drops and wait time.
  3. Put the card down, the sample well in the SAMPLE zone, a reagent-only well in the BLANK zone, and the package label (P-n) in view. Start capture before adding the reagent.
  4. Add the reagent. The app follows the reaction to the read time and checks liveness, blur, glare and light. For Test B it reads the lower liquid layer.
  5. The officer records what they see. The app shows positive, no colour change, or inconclusive, with the substances the colour is consistent with, then the next test the flow chart calls for.
  6. After the last test the app combines the results, for example "cocaine" after E3/E4, and warns if the NDPS quantity category can't be told in the field.
  7. Each result is signed on the phone's hardware key with time, GPS and officer, and chained. Nothing needs a network.
  8. Close: every phone's range and chain head go into the panchnama as a QR plus a 14-character code with a check character, and the witnesses sign.
  9. Generate the field-test report per package, the Form-1 item 5 text and the BSA s.63 certificate data. Sync when online; phones delete only after a durable checkpoint.

Physical card v2

The card is about 150 × 105 mm:

  • four 18 mm ArUco 4×4 markers, one per corner;
  • 24 colour patches of 10.5 mm;
  • a SAMPLE zone and a BLANK zone for a reagent-only well;
  • a package-label zone;
  • a 50 mm scale bar.

The printable file comes from tools/make_card.py, and the engine finds the card in its self-check. NCB's spot plate has three wells, so the final layout must follow the kit's real dimensions.

Real-kit validation protocol (answers "have you tested on real kits?")

  • Who: NCB or CRCL chemists, under their own licences. Our team never handles controlled substances.
  • What: NCB kits from 2–3 batches (one near expiry). About 7 reference substances the lab already holds, plus about 5 legal look-alikes and cutting agents, plus blanks. Three sample amounts (½, 1 and 2 match-heads) and one cut sample per substance.
  • Design: 12 substances × 3 replicates × 3 phones × 4 lights × 3 reading times = 1,296 captures, repeated at each amount for the Test A and E substances.
  • Blinding: 3–5 officers read each reaction by eye without knowing the substance. The app reads the same reactions. The chemist holds the key.
  • Pass criteria: false positives ≤ 1% proven at 95% confidence (≥ 299 negative controls with 0 false positives); inconclusive ≤ 15% outside night mode; app agreement with the lab's identity ≥ officers' visual agreement.
  • Before that, this week: card v2 with safe dyes on 3 phones, run through read_photos.py.

Confident in the design; real-world accuracy and the app not yet shown

Condition: no accuracy claim beyond "simulated" until real phones and NCB's reactions are tested. The app must exist before the finale: a public rival already demos one.

Phase 0 · Establish the problem

What NCB actually asked for

The statement's own words: field kits "rely on visual interpretation of a colour-change reaction", making results "subjective, difficult to standardise across officers", and leaving "no verifiable record that a test was actually conducted at a given place and time", so outcomes "cannot presently be relied upon as documentary evidence" [S1]. It asks for a mobile or web app for existing kits with no new hardware. The app must capture the result with a reference colour card in frame, classify it (positive, negative, inconclusive), create a tamper-evident record (timestamp, GPS, operator ID, image hash) and keep a searchable log. The result stays presumptive [S1].

Facts

  • The four required app functions and the "presumptive" note [S1]
  • Kits are used to form prima facie belief; every package is tested; the result goes in the panchnama; lab negatives after field positives happen [S4][S13]
  • Kit result is named in Rule 3(1), Rule 10(2) and Form-1 item 5; lab reports go to the Magistrate and IO within 15 days (Rule 14) [S5]
  • BSA s.63 certificate carries hash value and algorithm; BPR&D SOP asks for hashing on the phone [S7]
  • 1,15,236 NDPS cases in 2022; 30 NCB zonal units [S8]
  • SIMS Form F within 48 hours of FIR [S12]

Inferences (reasoned from facts)

  • "Documentary evidence" here means evidence of procedure, not substance identity
  • Poor lighting is common (night searches allowed; torches on the raid checklist)
  • No systematic field-vs-lab comparison exists today (no source shows one)
  • A wrong field classification can affect custody until the lab reports

Assumptions (we are betting on these)

  • Kit reaction colours are separable by a phone camera after calibration
  • Officers carry smartphones that pass attestation
  • NCB can share kit instruction sheets and supply reference cards with kits
  • Supervising officers have, or can get, Class 3 DSCs
  • Officers will adopt a tool that also creates records the defence can use

Unknown

  • Reagents in each bottle, read times, and the colours for Tests C and D (the kit chart in the NICFS guide gives Tests A, B and E)
  • API access to SIMS, e-Sakshya/ICJS and Parichay for NCB
  • Whether NCB uses e-Sakshya at all
  • Number of field users and phone models
  • How courts will treat this record type

Ambiguities, reasoned through

  • "Relied upon as documentary evidence." Evidence of what? Not substance identity: the handbook says only the notified lab expert's report proves that [S4], and the statement says the output doesn't replace lab testing [S1]. So we read it as documentary evidence of the procedure: that a test happened on this package, with this result, at this time and place, by this officer.
  • "Positive / negative / inconclusive." Positive for what? A kit step shows a colour pattern for a drug class. So the classifier answers "does this step's colour match outcome X in the kit profile", combined across steps as the kit sheet says. It never answers "this is heroin".
  • Which colour test Rule 10(2) means. Sampling happens before the Magistrate (Rule 9), usually after the field operation [S5]. Grouping may rely on the field records or on a re-test at sampling. The same app must serve both.
  • "Operator identifier." No identity scheme is specified. Parichay covers officials with government email [S22], but whether all seizing officers have it is unknown. The design needs a fallback: a device enrolment approved by a supervisor.
  • "Mobile/web." A web app can't use hardware-backed keys with attestation, or control camera exposure. That decides it for native Android.

Anchor format

The anchor is a QR code carrying, for every phone in the operation, the device ID, counter range and full SHA-256 chain head. It's printed or shown on screen and photographed onto the panchnama. A handwritten code of 14 base-32 characters (65 bits plus a check character, so a copying mistake reads as a typo, not as tampering) is also written in by the officer and signed by the witnesses. That's long enough that producing a different record set with the same code would take an infeasible number of hardware-rate-limited signatures. If a team phone can't join the closing step (dead battery), it writes its own code separately and the anchor records it as absent.

An offline verifier needs only three things: NCB's supervisor list, Google's attestation root and the anchor from the panchnama. It never trusts the server or our team.

Sync request flow

  1. Phone batches up to 100 unconfirmed records into an envelope (device ID, nonce, time, body hash), signs it with the device key, and adds a short-lived attested key bound to the server's latest nonce.
  2. Server: TLS → per-device rate limit → size and schema limits → envelope signature and device status → fresh attestation check.
  3. For each record: re-encode with JCS and require byte equality, so non-canonical input is rejected. Then verify the header signature, the payload hash and schema (time, GPS, officer), and the binding valid at the record's time; check counter and previous hash against the last stored record. A gap is accepted and flagged; a fork is rejected and alerted. Check the profile is known.
  4. Insert record and ledger entry in one transaction (idempotent); return per-record status, a signed receipt and the latest checkpoint.
  5. Phone uploads media with PUT /media/{sha256}; server streams to object storage, re-hashes, compares, and sets object lock.
  6. Phone deletes local copies only after the receipt, confirmed media and a durable checkpoint (taken after a backup) covering the ledger entry. Deleting on any checkpoint lost 5 of 16 records in a restore test (E14).

Authentication and authorisation

  • Enrolment (online, once): officer signs in (Parichay in production; agency directory in pilot). App generates an EC P-256 key in Android Keystore with an attestation challenge from the server. Server verifies the chain against Google's attestation roots and revocation list, plus verified boot, locked bootloader, security level and the challenge (attestation.py, E15); the app signing certificate and validity-date checks are designed but not yet implemented. The supervising officer then signs the binding (officer, device, key) with their own DSC; the server only stores and logs it. One active binding per device.
  • Field (offline): signing needs recent device authentication (PIN or biometric), so a stolen unlocked-then-locked phone can't sign.
  • Sync: TLS plus a device-signed request envelope (key ID, nonce, time, body hash). No bearer tokens stored on the phone.
  • Portal: SSO plus role, and attribute checks on agency, unit and case assignment. Roles: field officer, supervisor, IO / lab liaison, QA analyst (aggregates only), profile authority (two-person), auditor (read-only logs), system admin (operations, no case data).

Error handling: every sync item gets its own status, so one bad record never blocks the batch. Validation errors are explicit (SIGNATURE_INVALID, COUNTER_GAP, FORK, DEVICE_REVOKED, PROFILE_UNKNOWN). The phone retries with backoff and never deletes unconfirmed data.

Observability: sync success rate, forks, counter gaps, attestation failures, capture rejections by device model, abstention rate by profile, checkpoint export success. Logs go to the host's log system, kept 180 days in India [S18]. Alerts on forks, gaps and checkpoint failures.

Backup and disaster recovery: PostgreSQL WAL archiving plus nightly base backups to a second site; object-store replication; target RPO ≤ 15 minutes for the server. For records the effective loss is zero, because phones hold data until a durable checkpoint, taken after a backup, covers it (E14 restore test: 16/16 recovered). Quarterly restore drill.

  record_hash bytea PRIMARY KEY,             -- SHA-256 of the JCS header {device_id, counter, prev, payload_sha256}

Phase 11 · Government deployment reality

Could NCB actually adopt this?

  • Owner: NCB (operations plus IT). NCB already supplies kits free to agencies [S6], so it can also supply the reference card and publish profiles. The kit supplier prints the characterised card into every kit. This uses the existing supply chain and is not new hardware.
  • Shape: the engine, colour standard and report format ship as an embeddable SDK. e-Sakshya or an NCB app can host it; our app is the reference host. This answers "why not ask NIC to add a module?"
  • Hosting: NIC or MeitY-empanelled cloud in India; containers on VMs; nothing proprietary.
  • Interoperability: pre-fill SIMS Form F fields (a 48-hour duty the officer already has) [S12]. Deposit evidence bundles in e-Sakshya / Sakshya Lockers through ICJS where NCB has access [S14]. Parichay for identity. Access to these APIs is unverified.
  • Security review: designed for MASVS, ASVS and GIGW; threat model and SBOM ready for an auditor.
  • Migration: none needed. Today's field records are paper; the system starts fresh and links forward.
  • Training: one short module plus a practice mode using a practice card and dye cups.
  • Lock-in: open formats (JCS JSON, S3, PostgreSQL), open-source code (permissive licence), a documented verifier. NCB holds all signing keys.
  • If our team disappears: NCB owns code, keys and profiles; the verifier and format specification are public to the court process; any Java/Kotlin vendor can maintain a single Spring Boot app.
  • Cost drivers (sizing, not prices):
    • Pilot: 2 app VMs (about 4 vCPU / 16 GB each), 1 database VM, about 1 TB object storage plus backup.
    • Production: add a database replica, a DR copy, and roughly 1–3 TB/year of storage.
    • Per kit: one colour-controlled printed card.
    • The largest real cost is people: kit validation, profile governance, training. Prices depend on the host's tariff and aren't estimated here.
  • Procurement path: SIH win, then an MoU or pilot order with NCB (IP split per SIH guidelines [S2]), then a security audit, then hosting on NIC or empanelled cloud, then maintenance through NCB IT or an empanelled vendor. Inferred path; NCB's actual process is unknown.
  • Who operates it:
    • NCB HQ owns the system and publishes the supervisor list.
    • A profile authority of NCB-nominated chemists or forensic experts publishes kit profiles (two-person).
    • Zonal-unit supervisors enrol devices and handle revocation.
    • The host's operations team runs servers, backups and patches (no case-data access).
    • A small helpdesk handles training and device issues.

Phase 19 · Is this actually good?

Final checks

Would I approve this for a government deployment?

For a pilot, yes, with conditions: Phase-0 validation on real kits, NCB-owned signing keys, and a security audit. For national rollout, only after the pilot shows agreement with lab results and acceptable time per package.

Maintaining it for 5 years, what would I remove?

Anything that isn't the ledger, measurement, documents and QA. It's already down to one app, one server and one verifier. The next cut would be the portal UI in favour of reports, if NCB prefers.

Only 48 hours to show the core innovation?

record-core plus verifier CLI; the Android capture → sign → chain → anchor flow; card calibration on two vessels in one photo; the E3 tamper demo live. No server: export the bundle over USB.

Could we defend every claim to judges?

Yes, if we keep the labels honest: rules are verified; accuracy figures are simulated until the proxy study runs; kit separability is unverified. The most likely unanswerable question is "have you tested on real NCB kits?" The answer is no, and the plan for fixing that is Phase-0 validation.

Is there a simpler architecture?

Yes: approach A with no server (signed, anchored bundles copied to the unit PC). It solves the record problem. We didn't choose it because it doesn't meet the statement's classification requirement, and it can't provide enrolment trust or the lab loop. We keep it as the fallback mode.

Phase X · Convergence test

Why this is not just an AI-generated hackathon project

What 100 AI-assisted teams will probably build

  • Flutter or React Native app, or a web app
  • OpenCV colour matching, often a CNN/TFLite "drug classifier" trained on synthetic images with a high accuracy number
  • SHA-256 of the image, GPS, timestamp, QR code
  • Firebase/Supabase or FastAPI + PostgreSQL
  • Blockchain (Polygon/Hyperledger) "for immutability"
  • React dashboard with maps and heatmaps
  • NDPS law chatbot (LLM/RAG); PDF report; multilingual UI

Which of our parts are also generic

  • Android app, reference card (mandated), colour distance, hashing, GPS, PDF text, bilingual UI, a server with PostgreSQL
  • These are necessary but earn no points. Judges have seen them in every demo.

Our own "hackathon features" (demo appeal, low core value)

  • Accuracy dashboard charts: useful for NCB HQ, but in a demo it's synthetic data. One screen, clearly labelled.
  • Form-1 and panchnama text generation: real adoption value, but trivially copyable. Show in 20 seconds.
  • Kinetic colour curve: captured as data, but we make no claim from it until real data exists.

Obvious choices every AI agent would make, and we kept on purpose

  • PostgreSQL, a REST API, Docker, SHA-256. They're right for this load; being common isn't a reason to change them.
  • We differ where it counts: what the app claims (a colour group against a published standard, not a drug), how it captures (RAW first), who signs (hardware and supervisor keys, not the server), where trust anchors (panchnama and witnesses), and how decisions stay reproducible (no model on the phone).

Our key insight is that a colour test identifies a colour group, not a drug, and means something only against a documented standard; and that the record's value comes from binding it to the legally witnessed seizure, not from hashing a photo.
Our solution is fundamentally different because it makes the record set complete and reproducible: deletions and re-runs are detectable, and any expert can re-run the colour decision offline.
The hardest part of this problem is actually trust under adversarial conditions (a motivated officer, a sceptical defence expert, an insider admin), and being honest about what a colour can mean.
Most straightforward implementations would probably name a drug from a JPEG with a model, hash the image, and store it on a server or blockchain.
We instead sign on hardware keys, chain per device, anchor in the panchnama and video, and decide with deterministic measurement, because those are the only properties a court-side expert can check without trusting us.
This matters because NDPS cases are lost on procedure and no one knows how often kits are wrong. This makes the procedure provable and the kits measurable.

CORE INSIGHT: A field colour test identifies a colour group, not a drug, and only means something against a documented standard. India's courts found both the standard and a verifiable report missing. NCB's own kit says so: Test E's blue means cocaine or methaqualone.

CORE ADVANTAGE: Every kit test becomes a verifiable report, interpreted against a published, signed colour standard. An independent expert can check its completeness, integrity and colour decision offline, using only a phone, the existing kit and a printed card.

TECHNICAL DIFFERENTIATOR: Blank-relative colour bands built from NCB's kit chart (0.0% false positives across sample amounts in simulation), RAW-first capture, and hardware-signed, attested, per-device records carrying time, GPS and officer, anchored in the witness-signed panchnama (18/18 attacks caught).

OPERATIONAL DIFFERENTIATOR: It guides the officer through NCB's own kit, Tests A–E in the printed order, and slots into existing NDPS paperwork (Form-1 item 5, panchnama, Rule 10(2), SIMS Form F). It uses the Rule 14 lab report the IO already receives to measure kit accuracy.

WHY IT MATTERS: Seizure, remand and bail decisions before the lab report turn on this step. It turns the weakest, least-checked step of an NDPS case into one that can be proved and measured.

Honest status The earlier plan was not sufficiently differentiated: too many generic features. This version is differentiated on binding, completeness and reproducibility. Its biggest open risk is real kit data, which no amount of design can replace.
